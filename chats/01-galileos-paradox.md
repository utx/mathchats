# Galileo's Paradox

*Are there more whole numbers or even numbers? A puzzle about forever.*

"So do you know what a whole number is? 1, 2, 3. Any number that is whole. So a half isn't a whole number. 0.1 isn't a whole number. Got that, right?"

"So whole numbers would be 1, 2, 3, 4, 5... and on and on."

"Now even numbers, of course, are 2, 4, 6, 8... and on and on."

"So what do you think there are more of — whole numbers or even numbers?"

"Now, if you double any whole number, will it be even?"

"Think about it — double 1, double 2, double 10, double 27. Is it even?"

"So you can do that for any whole number, literally any of them, and the result will be even. So for every whole number there's a matching even number. The list could go on forever, right?"

"So how can there be more whole numbers than even numbers when they always match up?"

"What about square numbers? That's a number times itself. 3 times 3 is 9, so 9 is a square."

"There aren't so many squares, right? Between 1 and 100, how many squares can we think of? 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100. So there are way more whole numbers between 1 and 100 than squares."

"Between 100 and 200 there are even fewer — 121, 144, 169, 196 — right?"

"So are there more whole numbers or squares?"

"Well, every whole number has a square. You just times it by itself. So if you wrote out every whole number, you could always pair it with a square number."

"Weird, when you think about it. That's a paradox. Two things that seem undeniably true — that there should be more whole numbers than squares, and that every whole number has its own square — but they contradict each other. That's a paradox."

"A famous scientist called Galileo noticed this nearly 400 years ago. It puzzled him so much he decided that 'more' and 'fewer' just don't work properly when things go on forever."

"So what do you reckon? More whole numbers, or the same?"

## For the grown-ups

Galileo Galilei described this puzzle in *Two New Sciences* (1638), using the whole numbers and the square numbers. His conclusion was that words like "equal", "greater" and "less" can't be applied to infinite collections.

About 240 years later, Georg Cantor took the opposite view. He said two collections are the *same size* if you can pair them up one-to-one with nothing left over, which is exactly what doubling and squaring do. By that rule, the whole numbers, the even numbers and the squares are all the same size: each is "countably infinite". The surprising part is that a collection can be the same size as a piece of itself. That's actually one way mathematicians define what "infinite" means.

If they enjoyed this, a good follow-up is Hilbert's Hotel: a hotel with infinitely many rooms, all full, that can still fit in a new guest.
